Model Flow

What does a language model look like if you study it the way you would study a fluid: not molecule by molecule, but by how the whole body of it moves? Here, the molecules are the model's weights and circuits, and the fluid is thousands of texts flowing through the model's layers.

This is an exploratory project. It does not try to explain individual weights. It follows a population of texts through a model and measures where the population holds its shape and where it reorganizes. Most of the flow is smooth. Some apparent sharp changes turn out to be measurement artifacts. One large event, in Gemma 4, survives every control we tried, and we show how it happens.

One running total

A language model reads text as tokens (words or pieces of words) and turns each token into a vector, a list of a few thousand numbers. The vectors then pass through a stack of layers. Each layer reads the current vector, computes something nonlinear from it, and adds its result, its write, back on. The running total is called the residual stream (Elhage et al. 2021).

Each layer writes in two steps. Attention lets each token read from the tokens before it, through several parallel readers called heads. The MLP, a small two-stage network, then transforms each token's vector on its own. Both steps add their results to the residual stream.

Because every layer adds to the same running total, the vector after layer 9 and the vector after layer 18 live in the same space, with the same axes. That is what makes the fluid picture possible: one space, and a flow through it, layer by layer.

A cloud of texts

One text gives one point per layer. We send thousands of short encyclopedia articles (DBpedia summaries, at most 64 tokens) through an already-trained model, without changing its weights (a frozen model), and represent each article at each layer by the average of its token vectors. That gives a cloud of points at every layer. The question is how the cloud changes from one layer to the next. Where something is fitted to articles (a grouping, a map between layers), it is tested on held-out articles that the fit never saw.

We compare two layers in two ways:

  • Place. Does an article keep its coordinates? We take the cosine between its vectors at the two layers, after subtracting each layer's average vector: the centered cosine. 1 means the same direction, 0 means unrelated.
  • Company. Does an article keep its neighbours? We list its 10 nearest articles at each layer and count how many are shared. This ignores the axes entirely: if the whole cloud rotated, every article would keep all its neighbours.

Models: Gemma 4 E2B and E4B, Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3.5-2B, SmolLM2-1.7B, Pythia-410M (untrained and trained), and one encoder, ModernBERT-large. All public, all frozen.

What we see

Comparing every pair of layers gives a map of the whole model. In Gemma 4 E2B the map has two features. Dark square blocks are stretches of layers that share coordinates. And at layer 14 there is a sharp break: across that one layer, articles' coordinates become unrelated. We call a layer like that a turn.

Other models show breaks too, if measured plainly: Qwen3 seems to turn at layer 2 (centered cosine 0.18) and SmolLM2 at layer 7 (0.25). Before reading anything into these, each needs a reference.

Is it real? Three controls

An untrained model: staying put is the default

In an untrained Pythia (choose "pythia_0" above), every article still finds itself at any layer up to six layers away, and keeps 68% of its neighbours at that distance. Each layer adds a small random write to a large running total, so nothing moves far quickly. So blocks of shared coordinates are not, by themselves, something the model learned (Veit et al. 2016). Training shows up as departures from this.

Robust scaling: a change in a few huge coordinates can look like a turn of the whole cloud

Language models carry a handful of coordinates with values hundreds of times larger than the rest (Kovaleva et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2024). When one switches on, it dominates every cosine. Rescale each coordinate by its typical spread, and the Qwen3 and SmolLM2 breaks become ordinary layers (0.69 and 0.85). Qwen3, Qwen3.5 and SmolLM2 then keep their coordinates all the way through. Gemma's turn stays (0.05).

The huge coordinates are not noise. (Added 18 October.) In Qwen3, three coordinates switch on at layer 2, almost entirely on the first token of each text, which becomes the token every other token can attend to by default (an attention sink, Sun et al. 2024). Blocking that switch breaks the model. But the other 2,045 coordinates keep their arrangement across layer 2. So the event is real and needed, and it is a change in a few coordinates of one token, not a turn of the whole cloud like Gemma's.

A second model as reference: rotations link layers, and they link models

To ask whether information survives a layer, fit a rotation from one layer's cloud to another's on some articles, then check whether it carries new articles to themselves. It does, very well. The same procedure works almost as well between two different models.

Both results point the same way: the arrangement of articles is shared, between layers of one model and between different models (Huh et al. 2024), and a rotation carries one onto the other.

The second model also sets a limit on what a good fit means. Two different models were never rotations of each other, yet one fits onto the other almost as well as one layer onto the next. So a good fit between two layers does not show that one layer rotated the other. It shows only that both arrange the articles in a similar way (Ding et al. 2021). And the rotation carries the arrangement, not everything: about half of each vector's length is left unexplained.

What survives: Gemma 4's turn, up close

Across layers 13 to 15 of Gemma 4 E2B, articles' coordinates become unrelated (centered cosine 0.004), yet each article keeps about half of its 10 neighbours, where neighbouring layers elsewhere keep about four in five. The turn holds on a second sample, at 1,000 to 6,000 articles, with the huge coordinates removed, in 32-bit and 16-bit arithmetic, and for single tokens as well as whole articles.

To see the turn as a flow, we need one more idea. At each layer we sort the articles into a few groups with k-means, a standard method that splits points into a chosen number of groups by distance, using no labels. k-means starts from random points, so we run it twice and score how well the two runs agree: the repeatability (1 = identical, 0 = chance). Then we follow the same articles from one layer's groups to the next.

Most of the depth is steady flow: groups carry over from layer to layer. At layers 12–16 the carry-over drops and topic agreement roughly halves (lower panel), then the groups re-form by layer 17. The map shows who moves with whom, not why. Its groups are our choice of resolution, not the model's concepts.

How Gemma turns

After each layer, Gemma 4 multiplies the whole residual stream by a fixed number stored in the checkpoint, the layer scalar (layer_scalar). Gemma's technical report says it is there to keep values within 16-bit range (Gemma Team 2026). Unlike similar tricks that scale only a layer's write (Touvron et al. 2021), it scales the running total too.

  • Two layers out-write the stream. Layers 13 and 14 write vectors 14 and 40 times the size of the residual, at right angles to it. Their scalars (0.07 and 0.05) then shrink the sum. The old residual becomes a small remainder next to the new write, so the coordinates turn. Nothing can be deleted from a running total; writing something much larger is the way to overwrite it, an idea proposed for GPT-2 by Heimersheim & Turner 2023.
  • The scalar sets how sharp the turn is. Multiply the scalars of layers 13–14 by a factor and the turn changes smoothly: smaller scalars give a sharper turn, and with both scalars set to 1 the turn across layer 14 is about that of an ordinary layer.
  • The MLPs make most of the write. Replacing the MLP output of layer 13 or 14 with its average moves the turn to the next layers rather than removing it. Removing single attention heads barely matters. (The section on neurons below adds a qualification.)

Neurons and the turn

Everything above is measured on whole clouds of texts, never on single units of the model. A recent essay argues that single units are more meaningful than usually assumed: most neurons in modern language models do one main job (Cammarata 2026). So it is fair to ask whether Gemma's turn is something a few neurons do.

A neuron here is one of the MLP's inner units. At every token, each MLP layer computes a few thousand values, one per neuron (6,144 in Gemma 4 E2B at layers 13 and 14). It then adds to the running total a fixed direction per neuron, scaled by that neuron's value. The sum of those scaled directions is the MLP's write. We asked four questions, and wrote down what we expected before each test (notes, with every result and the tests that failed).

Can people read what the turn's neurons do?

If the turn is a few interpretable units, its neurons should be easier to label than random ones. Two independent raters (language models, not told which neurons were which) saw the six tokens where each neuron is most positive and the six where it is most negative, for the 16 turn neurons whose writes vary most, mixed with 16 random ones. Both rated the turn's neurons as more readable, and judged between a third and a half of them to do one nameable job, against two or three of the random ones. One example: a layer-14 neuron that fires strongly negative on the first digit of a year in a date ("on October 31, 1899"). So the neurons are partly readable. That is weaker than "clean", and it was judged on each neuron's strongest examples, where neurons look their best.

Does a small set of neurons make the write?

If so, a handful of neurons should account for most of each token's write. We counted how many neurons, taken largest first, it takes to make up half of the write, and compared the turn layers with every other layer of Gemma and of Qwen3-1.7B. At the turn it takes around 1–2% of a layer's neurons, which is ordinary for a middle layer in both models. The turn's write is not unusually concentrated, and no small set of neurons is shared across tokens.

Does the turn need the right content, or only a large write?

The coordinate turn is measured with a cosine, and a cosine only sees whether the axes changed, not what was written. So we also track company, as defined above: for each token, which 10 others are closest after layer 14, and whether they are the same ones as in the unchanged model. We then changed the writes of layers 13 and 14 in six ways, from setting 1,024 random neurons to their average value up to replacing the whole write with noise of the same size.

Read the left panel down the rows. The blue bars do not move: every change, even noise, leaves the turn as it was. The orange and green bars fall as more of the content is replaced, down to almost no company kept when the write is another text's or noise. So the size of the write sets the turn, and its content sets the company. The neuron swaps on Gemma 4 E4B, the larger model, also leave its turn in place. In the unchanged model, about a third of each token's neighbours survive the turn although the axes change completely, the token-level version of the article example above.

Where does the size come from?

A layer's own scalar cannot turn it: multiplying everything by one number leaves a cosine unchanged. But the scalar shrinks the running total that the next layer writes onto. If that is part of the turn, changing layer 12's scalar should change the turn at layer 13. It does, smoothly (right panel): a smaller scalar gives a larger turn, and a larger scalar a smaller one. Even at four times the trained value, layer 13 turns more than ordinary layers, so the size of its own write does the rest.

One result we did not expect: with every neuron at layers 13 and 14 set to its average value, the MLPs add the same vector to every token, which cannot turn anything by itself, and the turn still happens. Whatever else writes at those layers is then large enough against the shrunken total. We have not yet separated attention from Gemma's per-layer embedding inputs there.

Does the map tell us anything about neurons?

  • Partly. Where the map splits a group of articles at a layer, that layer's neurons separate the two halves somewhat better than neurons two layers earlier, and pairs of groups that were already apart show no such gain. The effect is small.
  • No: neurons in neighbouring layers that fire together do not read the same direction of the running total, so a label does not carry from one layer to the next.
  • No: dividing a neuron's write by the size of the running total did not predict how much removing it hurts the model better than its plain activation size.

What this means. Neurons at the turn are somewhat more readable than random ones, and the arrangement of tokens lives in what they write. The turn itself is not a circuit of a few neurons. It is a size event: a large write landing on a running total that the previous layer's scalar has made small. This does not contradict the essay, which is about what neurons mean, not about where large-scale events in the flow come from. It does say where not to look for them.

A prediction on a second model

In Gemma 4 E2B, the smaller a layer's scalar, the sharper its turn (rank correlation 0.86). So before computing any geometry of the larger Gemma 4 E4B, we read only its stored scalars and wrote down three predictions: its two sharpest turns lie in layers 22–24; the scalar–turn correlation is at least 0.5; a block of shared coordinates starts at layer 23–25. All three held: sharpest turns at 23 and 22, correlation 0.90, a block starting at 23. pre-registered · held-out model

Caveats: the prediction window left out layer 1 (third-smallest scalar), a choice made after reading the scalars. Both models are Gemma 4. A scalar meant to keep values in range is expected to be small where a layer writes something huge; the test shows that this numerical device also marks the geometry. The causal check on E4B has not been run.

The mental map, provisionally

This is the picture we now work from. It summarizes these measurements on these models; it is not a law.

  • The residual stream is one space, but not a fixed coordinate system for meaning. Each layer is a nonlinear update that can shift the coordinates.
  • Mostly the shift is gradual. Stretches of layers share coordinates closely, partly because a running total resists change even before training.
  • Some shifts are sudden. In Gemma 4 a layer can re-express the whole cloud at once, keeping much of who sits near whom but changing the axes. It does so by out-writing the stream, with a stored scalar setting how sharp the change is.
  • Across a shift the arrangement survives and a rotation carries it over. So layers can be compared with measures that ignore the axes (neighbours, groups), directly within a stretch that shares coordinates, or after fitting a rotation.

What did not hold

  • An attention head was expected to drive the turn. Knocking out Gemma's layer-14 induction head (a head that looks back for an earlier copy of the current token and reads what followed it), or any single head tried, leaves it unchanged.
  • Blocks were expected to predict which layers can be skipped. Skip damage follows how much a layer writes instead.
  • Layers were expected to settle bottom-up during training pre-registered. In Pythia-410M the ends settled first and the middle last.

Limits

  • One family turns. Why the others do not is open; Gemma's scalars and its reuse of earlier layers' attention inputs (the shaded layers in the track figure) are the remaining candidates.
  • Small models (0.4B–4B), short texts (64 tokens), one corpus. Article vectors are token averages, so they partly reflect which words a text contains.
  • Our first Gemma token-level runs dropped the beginning-of-text token Gemma expects. Re-extracted, the turn is unchanged.
  • Pre-registrations are self-dated in the project's version history, not externally timestamped.

Where this goes next

If the arrangement is shared and a rotation carries it, a natural next question is what the rotated states are good for, within one model and between models. That is the subject of ongoing work. A practical side result, a way to pick an embedding layer without labels, is still being tested and will be written up separately.

Reproducing

Every number on this page is listed in a claims ledger with the result file it comes from and the script that produced it, and a checker re-derives each one from the results. Code, pre-registrations, results and the scripts to re-extract every feature cache are in the repository, archived with a DOI.

No model was trained. The project used about 100 GPU-hours (two NVIDIA P100s and one RTX 3060) and about 330 CPU job-hours. It cached about 0.6 TB of hidden states: some 205 million per-layer vectors from frozen public models.
