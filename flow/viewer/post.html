Model Flow

What does a language model look like if you study it the way you would study a fluid: not molecule by molecule, but by how the whole body of it moves? Here, the molecules are the model's weights and circuits, and the fluid is thousands of texts flowing through the model's layers.

This is an exploratory project. It does not try to explain individual weights. It follows a population of texts through a model and measures where the population holds its shape and where it reorganizes. Most of the flow is smooth. Some apparent sharp changes turn out to be measurement artifacts. One large event, in Gemma 4, survives every control we tried, and we show how it happens.

One running total

A language model reads text as tokens (words or pieces of words) and turns each token into a vector, a list of a few thousand numbers. The vectors then pass through a stack of layers. Each layer reads the current vector, computes something nonlinear from it, and adds its result, its write, back on. The running total is called the residual stream (Elhage et al. 2021).

Because every layer adds to the same running total, the vector after layer 9 and the vector after layer 18 live in the same space, with the same axes. That is what makes the fluid picture possible: one space, and a flow through it, layer by layer.

A cloud of texts

One text gives one point per layer. We send thousands of short encyclopedia articles (DBpedia summaries, at most 64 tokens) through a frozen, already-trained model, and represent each article at each layer by the average of its token vectors. That gives a cloud of points at every layer. The question is how the cloud changes from one layer to the next.

We compare two layers in two ways:

  • Place. Does an article keep its coordinates? We take the cosine between its vectors at the two layers, after subtracting each layer's average vector: the centered cosine. 1 means the same direction, 0 means unrelated.
  • Company. Does an article keep its neighbours? We list its 10 nearest articles at each layer and count how many are shared. This ignores the axes entirely: if the whole cloud rotated, every article would keep all its neighbours.

Models: Gemma 4 E2B and E4B, Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3.5-2B, SmolLM2-1.7B, Pythia-410M (untrained and trained), and one encoder, ModernBERT-large. All public, all frozen.

What we see

Comparing every pair of layers gives a map of the whole model. In Gemma 4 E2B the map has two features. Dark square blocks are stretches of layers that share coordinates. And at layer 14 there is a sharp break: across that one layer, articles' coordinates become unrelated. We call a layer like that a turn.

Other models show breaks too, if measured plainly: Qwen3 seems to turn at layer 2 (centered cosine 0.18) and SmolLM2 at layer 7 (0.25). Before reading anything into these, each needs a reference.

Is it real? Three controls

An untrained model: staying put is the default

In an untrained Pythia (choose "pythia_0" above), every article still finds itself at any layer up to six layers away, and keeps 68% of its neighbours at that distance. Each layer adds a small random write to a large running total, so nothing moves far quickly. So blocks of shared coordinates are not, by themselves, something the model learned (Veit et al. 2016). Training shows up as departures from this.

Robust scaling: a few huge coordinates can fake a turn

Language models carry a handful of coordinates with values hundreds of times larger than the rest (Kovaleva et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2024). When one switches on, it dominates every cosine. Rescale each coordinate by its typical spread, and the Qwen3 and SmolLM2 breaks become ordinary layers (0.69 and 0.85). Qwen3, Qwen3.5 and SmolLM2 then keep their coordinates all the way through. Gemma's turn stays (0.05).

A second model: rotations link layers, and they link models

To ask whether information survives a layer, fit a rotation from one layer's cloud to another's on some articles, then check whether it carries new articles to themselves. It does, very well. The same procedure works almost as well between two different models.

Both results say the same thing: the arrangement of articles is shared, between layers of one model and between models (Huh et al. 2024), and a rotation carries one onto the other. What the cross-model result rules out is a narrower reading: a good fit alone cannot tell "this layer rotated the last one" apart from "these are two good encodings of the same articles" (Ding et al. 2021). A rotation also carries the arrangement, not everything: about half of each vector's length is left unexplained.

What survives: Gemma 4's turn, up close

Across layers 13 to 15 of Gemma 4 E2B, articles' coordinates become unrelated (centered cosine 0.004), yet each article keeps about half of its 10 neighbours (0.49, against 0.81 between neighbouring layers elsewhere). The turn holds on a second sample, at 1,000 to 6,000 articles, with the huge coordinates removed, in 32-bit and 16-bit arithmetic, and for single tokens as well as whole articles.

To see the turn as a flow, we need one more idea. At each layer we sort the articles into a few groups with k-means, using no labels. k-means starts from random points, so we run it twice and score how well the two runs agree: the repeatability (1 = identical, 0 = chance). Then we follow the same articles from one layer's groups to the next.

Most of the depth is steady flow: groups carry over from layer to layer. At layers 12–16 the carry-over drops and topic agreement falls from 0.50 to 0.26 (eight groups), then the groups re-form by layer 17. The map shows who moves with whom, not why. Its groups are our choice of resolution, not the model's concepts.

How Gemma turns

After each layer, Gemma 4 multiplies the whole residual stream by a fixed number stored in the checkpoint, the layer scalar (layer_scalar). Gemma's technical report says it is there to keep values within 16-bit range (Gemma Team 2026). Unlike similar tricks that scale only a layer's write (Touvron et al. 2021), it scales the running total too.

  • Two layers out-write the stream. Layers 13 and 14 write vectors 14 and 40 times the size of the residual, at right angles to it. Their scalars (0.07 and 0.05) then shrink the sum. The old residual becomes a small remainder next to the new write, so the coordinates turn. Nothing can be deleted from a running total; writing something much larger is the way to overwrite it, an idea proposed for GPT-2 by Heimersheim & Turner 2023.
  • The scalar sets the turn. Multiply the scalars of layers 13–14 by a factor and the turn changes smoothly: centered cosine across layer 14 goes from 0.02 (×0.5) to 0.04 (as trained), 0.08 (×2), 0.17 (×4) and 0.48 (scalars set to 1).
  • The MLPs make the write. Replacing the MLP output of layer 13 or 14 with its average moves the turn to the next layers rather than removing it. Attention heads barely matter.

A prediction on a second model

In Gemma 4 E2B, the smaller a layer's scalar, the sharper its turn (rank correlation 0.86). So before computing any geometry of the larger Gemma 4 E4B, we read only its stored scalars and wrote down three predictions: its two sharpest turns lie in layers 22–24; the scalar–turn correlation is at least 0.5; a block of shared coordinates starts at layer 23–25. All three held: sharpest turns at 23 and 22, correlation 0.90, a block starting at 23. pre-registered · held-out model

Caveats: the prediction window left out layer 1 (third-smallest scalar), a choice made after reading the scalars. Both models are Gemma 4. A scalar meant to keep values in range is expected to be small where a layer writes something huge; the test shows that this numerical device also marks the geometry. The causal check on E4B has not been run.

The mental map, provisionally

This is the picture we now work from. It summarizes these measurements on these models; it is not a law.

  • The residual stream is one space, but not a fixed coordinate system for meaning. Each layer is a nonlinear update that can shift the coordinates.
  • Mostly the shift is gradual. Stretches of layers share coordinates closely, partly because a running total resists change even before training.
  • Some shifts are sudden. In Gemma 4 a layer can re-express the whole cloud at once, keeping much of who sits near whom but changing the axes. It does so by out-writing the stream, with a stored scalar setting how sharp the change is.
  • Across a shift the arrangement survives and a rotation carries it over. So layers can be compared with measures that ignore the axes (neighbours, groups), directly within a stretch that shares coordinates, or after fitting a rotation.

What did not hold

  • An attention head was expected to drive the turn. Knocking out Gemma's layer-14 induction head, or any single head tried, leaves it unchanged.
  • Blocks were expected to predict which layers can be skipped. Skip damage follows how much a layer writes instead.
  • Layers were expected to settle bottom-up during training pre-registered. In Pythia-410M the ends settled first and the middle last.

Limits

  • One family turns. Why the others do not is open; Gemma's scalars and its key-value sharing are the remaining candidates.
  • Small models (0.4B–4B), short texts (64 tokens), one corpus. Article vectors are token averages, so they partly reflect which words a text contains.
  • Our first Gemma token-level runs dropped the beginning-of-text token Gemma expects. Re-extracted, the turn is unchanged.
  • Pre-registrations are self-dated in the project's version history, not externally timestamped.

Where this goes next

If the arrangement is shared and a rotation carries it, a natural next question is what the rotated states are good for, within one model and between models. That is the subject of ongoing work. A practical side result, a way to pick an embedding layer without labels, is still being tested and will be written up separately.

Reproducing

Every number on this page is listed in a claims ledger with the result file it comes from and the script that produced it, and a checker re-derives each one from the results. Code, pre-registrations, results and the scripts to re-extract every feature cache are in the repository, archived with a DOI.

No model was trained. The project used about 100 GPU-hours (two NVIDIA P100s and one RTX 3060) and about 330 CPU job-hours. It cached about 0.6 TB of hidden states: some 205 million per-layer vectors from frozen public models.
